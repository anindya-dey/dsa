---
title: "Message Queues & Pub/Sub"
summary: "Put work in a durable pile and let someone else deal with it later, so producers and consumers never have to be running, fast, or sized like each other."
---

Imagine a restaurant with the kitchen visible from the counter. If the
cashier must stand at the kitchen window and wait for each dish before taking
the next order, the whole restaurant runs at the kitchen's speed. The fix
invented by every real restaurant: the ticket rail. The cashier clips
orders to the rail and moves on. The kitchen pulls tickets at its own pace.
The rush at lunch doesn't have to match the kitchen's capacity, because the
rail absorbs the difference.

A message queue is that rail, and it buys three separate things at once.

## What you actually buy

**Decoupling in time (async work).** The producer doesn't wait. An
e-commerce site can accept 1000 orders per second even if payment processing
handles 200 per second, because orders wait in the queue rather than at the
checkout. The user experience stops being chained to the slowest step.

**Decoupling in space.** Producers don't need to know who consumes the
message. Order events from the storefront can be consumed by shipping,
analytics, fraud, and email, without the storefront knowing any of them
exist. Consumers come and go, and the producer never changes.

**Surviving crashes.** A durable queue holds messages on disk and
redelivers them if a consumer fails before finishing. If your email service
is down for an hour, the emails queue up rather than disappear. Compare with
a direct HTTP call, where a crash means a lost message and an angry user.

The difference from a database is worth noting: queues are optimized for
**sequential processing of events as they arrive**, with each message
typically consumed once and deleted, and no ad-hoc querying of the past.
(When you need to replay and reprocess history, that's
[event sourcing](/system-design/fundamentals/event-sourcing-and-cqrs).)

## Queue vs. pub/sub: two shapes of the same idea

People use the terms interchangeably, but there's a crisp difference in
*who gets a copy*:

- **Point-to-point queue**: each message goes to **one** consumer. Ten
  workers can share one queue; the queue hands each message to one of them.
  The work is divided. This is task distribution: "someone process this
  order".
- **Pub/sub (topics)**: each message is delivered to **every subscriber** of
  the topic. The same "order-placed" event goes to shipping AND analytics
  AND fraud, each with their own copy. This is broadcast: "everyone who
  cares, here's what happened".

The rule of thumb: use a queue when the message is **work to be done**, and
a topic when the message is **a fact that happened**. "Send this email" is
work (one sender suffices). "An order was placed" is a fact (everyone
interested should hear it).

Kafka blurs the line deliberately: it's a distributed log where consumers
track their own position, so one consumer group behaves like a queue (each
message processed once by the group), while multiple groups each get the
full stream like pub/sub.

## The delivery guarantees trilemma

This is where design questions get sharp. When a consumer reads a message,
processes it, and crashes before acknowledging, does the message come back?
The options:

- **At-most-once**: deliver and delete. Fastest, but a crash loses the
  message.
- **At-least-once**: delete only after the consumer acknowledges. A crash
  means redelivery. Safe, but now the same message can arrive twice.
- **Exactly-once**: what everyone wants, and what doesn't exist for free.
  Systems get close by combining at-least-once delivery with
  [idempotent processing](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once):
  process it as though it might be a duplicate, because it might be.

At-least-once plus idempotent consumers is the standard production answer.
Which brings up a subtle and important point: **a message queue by itself
does not give you transactions with the database**. The classic bug is:
consumer receives message, writes to the database, crashes before
acknowledging. Redelivery: the database gets written twice. The clean fix
is the [transactional outbox](/system-design/fundamentals/distributed-transactions),
and every serious event-driven system grows one eventually.

## Backpressure and what happens when the kitchen falls behind

The ticket rail has a physical limit; so does a queue. When producers
outrun consumers for long enough, the queue grows without bound, and you
face the choice of the [backpressure](/system-design/fundamentals/backpressure-and-flow-control)
problem: reject, buffer, or shed load. A queue doesn't make the problem
disappear, it gives you a place to make the decision deliberately. Long
queue depth is also a monitoring signal: a queue that keeps growing is a
system telling you its consumers can't keep up.
