Containers solved *packaging* (an app plus its dependencies in one
portable image), and that created a *scheduling* problem: hundreds of
images, thousands of machines, and a constant churn of deploys,
crashes, and scaling, who decides which container runs where, and
keeps it running? **Orchestration** is that answer, and Kubernetes is
the industry's default. This page is the *concepts* a system designer
needs: the object model, the scheduling and self-healing loop, and
the operational shape, without the YAML.

## The object model

Kubernetes's vocabulary, as the concepts they represent:

- **Pod** (the unit of scheduling: one or more containers that share
  a network namespace and lifecycle, usually one container per pod;
  the *atom* the scheduler places).
- **Deployment** (the *desired state*: "run N replicas of this
  image"; the controller reconciles reality to it, the
  [reconciliation loop](#the-reconciliation-loop), which is the whole
  idea).
- **Service** (a stable virtual IP and DNS name in front of a
  changing set of pods, the [load
  balancer](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing) and
  [service discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_discovery),
  because pods come and go and their IPs are ephemeral).
- **Ingress** (HTTP routing from outside to the services inside, the
  [API gateway](/system-design/fundamentals/api-gateway-and-service-mesh)
  layer, cluster-shaped).
- **ConfigMap / Secret** (configuration and credentials injected at
  runtime, not baked into images, the
  [secrets-management](/system-design/fundamentals/security-fundamentals)
  discipline).
- **Namespace** (a logical partition of the cluster: teams, or
  environments, the [multi-tenancy](/system-design/fundamentals/multi-tenancy-and-data-residency)
  primitive).

The unifying idea: **you declare the desired state, and controllers
make it true.** You never "start a container"; you write "I want 3
replicas", and the system works to keep it so.

## The reconciliation loop (the core concept)

Every Kubernetes controller is the same loop: **observe the actual
state, compare it to the desired state, and take action to close the
gap.** The deployment controller sees "3 wanted, 2 running" and
starts one; sees a crashed pod and replaces it; sees a new image and
rolls the pods over. This is the
[control-loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_loop) idea, and
it is why Kubernetes is *self-healing*: it does not need to be told
about failures, because it is *always* comparing desired to actual.

The design consequences: **declarative, not imperative** (you state
the goal, not the steps), **level-triggered, not edge-triggered**
(the controller reacts to the *current* state, not to *events*, so a
missed event is harmless, because the next loop catches it: the
[eventual-consistency](/system-design/fundamentals/cap-theorem-and-consistency-models)
discipline, and the reason it is robust), and **idempotent actions**
(doing the reconciliation twice is fine, the
[idempotency](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once)
discipline, everywhere).

## Scheduling and self-healing

The **scheduler** places each pod on a node: it filters (which nodes
can run this, resources, affinity, taints) and scores (which is
best), then binds. The concepts: **resource requests and limits**
(the [capacity-planning](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
contract, a pod declares what it needs, and the scheduler respects
it), **affinity/anti-affinity** (place these together / spread these
apart, the [multi-region](/system-design/fundamentals/multi-region-and-active-active)
"spread across failure domains" idea, at node scale), and **taints
and tolerations** (dedicated nodes: "only GPU workloads here", the
[GPU-management](/ai-ml/systems/gpu-accelerator-management)
isolation, cluster-shaped).

The **self-healing** machinery: liveness probes (restart a hung
container, the [health-check](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing)
idea, at the container), readiness probes (do not send traffic to a
not-yet-ready pod, the [draining](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing)
discipline), and the replica-set controller (replace a dead pod).
The honest framing: **Kubernetes does not prevent failure; it
automates recovery**, which is the
[availability](/system-design/fundamentals/availability-and-reliability)
philosophy in platform form.

## The operational shape

- **Deploys** are *rollouts*: a Deployment with a new image rolls
  pods gradually (max-surge, max-unavailable), with automatic
  rollback on a failed health check, the
  [canary/rolling-deploy](/system-design/fundamentals/reliability-engineering)
  discipline, built in.
- **Scaling** is Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA: scale replicas on
  CPU/custom metrics, the
  [autoscaling](/ai-ml/systems/autoscaling-inference) idea,
  cluster-wide) and Cluster Autoscaler (add nodes when pods cannot
  be scheduled).
- **Stateless by default**: pods are cattle (killed and replaced
  freely), so *state* lives outside, in a
  [database](/system-design/fundamentals/database-fundamentals), an
  [object store](/system-design/fundamentals/object-storage), or a
  [cache](/system-design/fundamentals/caching), which is *why*
  Kubernetes fits the stateless tier so well and stateful systems
  need care (StatefulSets, operators).
- **The cost of the abstraction**: a Kubernetes cluster is itself a
  distributed system to operate (the control plane, the networking,
  the storage, the [complexity](/system-design/fundamentals/microservices-vs-monolith)
  trade, platform-shaped), which is why "do you need Kubernetes?" is
  a real question for small systems (a managed platform or a couple
  of VMs may be the honest answer).
