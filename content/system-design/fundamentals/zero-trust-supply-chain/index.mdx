---
title: "Zero Trust & Supply Chain Security"
summary: "Assume-breach networking and securing the software you depend on."
---

Classic security drew a perimeter: a trusted inside (the corporate
network, the VPC) and an untrusted outside, with a firewall between.
Two shifts broke that model: **the perimeter dissolved** (services in
the cloud, remote work, and lateral movement mean an attacker who
gets *one* foothold is *inside*), and **the software itself became a
supply chain** (your system is mostly other people's code, and
attacking *them* attacks you). The two modern answers are **zero
trust** (stop trusting the network; authenticate every request) and
**supply-chain security** (stop trusting your dependencies;
verify what you build and ship).

## Zero trust: never trust, always verify

The premise: **the network is not a security boundary.** Being "inside
the VPC" grants *nothing*; every request, from a user, from a
service, from "inside", must be authenticated and authorized on its
own merits. The principles:

- **Verify explicitly** (authenticate and authorize *every* request
  from *every* source: the [auth](/system-design/fundamentals/auth-basics)
  discipline, with no "trusted network" exemptions, a service
  authenticates as *itself* (the workload identity), and a request
  carries its proof).
- **Least privilege** (each service, each identity, gets the minimum
  access, the [security-fundamentals](/system-design/fundamentals/security-fundamentals)
  principle, applied *service to service*, not just at the edge:
  the reason [service meshes](/system-design/fundamentals/api-gateway-and-service-mesh)
  and [mTLS](/system-design/fundamentals/networking-basics) matter,
every internal hop is authenticated, so a compromised service
  cannot freely call its neighbors).
- **Assume breach** (design as if the attacker is *already inside*:
  segment the network so lateral movement is hard, encrypt internal
  traffic, and log everything, the
  [observability](/system-design/fundamentals/observability) that
  detects the *unexpected* call).

The systems consequences: **mTLS everywhere** (not just at the edge:
the internal mesh's default), **service identity** (short-lived
credentials, workload identities, the
[auth](/system-design/fundamentals/auth-basics) token discipline, for
machines), and **micro-segmentation** (services can reach only what
they must, which is a *policy* the [mesh](/system-design/fundamentals/api-gateway-and-service-mesh)
enforces, the "least privilege" of the network, made real).

## Supply chain: your dependencies are your attack surface

The insight behind every recent high-profile breach: **you ship other
people's code, and if you do not know *what* you shipped, you shipped
whatever the attacker wanted.** The attack vectors: a **compromised
dependency** (a malicious version of a library you install), a
**typosquat** (a package named almost like the real one), a
**compromised build** (the CI pipeline itself attacked, the
SolarWinds shape), and a **vulnerable transitive dependency** (a flaw
in something your dependency depends on, three levels down).

The defenses, and the artifacts:

- **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** (a machine-readable *inventory*
  of every component in your artifact: what you shipped, and
  therefore what you must patch when a CVE lands, the
  [lineage](/ai-ml/systems/data-versioning-lineage) discipline,
  for software supply chains).
- **Dependency scanning and pinning** (scan dependencies for known
  vulnerabilities continuously, and *pin* versions (lockfiles) so a
  build is reproducible and a silent upgrade cannot inject a
  compromised version, the [reproducibility](/ai-ml/systems/reproducibility-metadata)
  discipline, security-shaped).
- **Signed artifacts and provenance** (sign the build output, and
  record *how* it was built (the [SLSA](https://slsa.dev/) framework:
  a verifiable chain from source to artifact), so a tampered build
  is *detectable*, and "where did this binary come from" has a
  cryptographic answer).
- **Least-privilege builds** (the CI/CD pipeline has minimal
  credentials: a compromised build step cannot exfiltrate the
  production keys, the [secrets](/system-design/fundamentals/security-fundamentals)
  discipline, and the containment that limits a build compromise).

## Why it is a systems topic

Both are *architectural*, not product features: zero trust changes
*how services authenticate and communicate* (every internal call is
authenticated, a design decision, not a setting), and supply-chain
security changes *how you build and ship* (SBOMs, signing, and
provenance are pipeline *architecture*). They also both follow the
[defense-in-depth](/system-design/fundamentals/security-fundamentals)
principle: no single control is trusted, so the network is segmented
*and* every request is authenticated *and* the artifact is signed
*and* the build is least-privilege, so one broken layer is an
incident, not a breach. The honest framing for a design review:
"what is the blast radius if *this* component is compromised?" is the
question both practices answer, and it belongs in the design, not
the post-incident review.
