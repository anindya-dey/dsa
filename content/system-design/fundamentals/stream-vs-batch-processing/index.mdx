---
title: "Stream vs. Batch Processing"
summary: "Data as an unbounded, ever-arriving flow rather than a bounded table: windows, watermarks, and what exactly-once actually costs."
---

The default mental model of data is a *table*: a bounded set of rows,
finished, sitting still, ready to be queried whenever. Stream processing
starts from the opposite model: data as an **unbounded, continuously
arriving flow of events** (the event sourcing
[log](/system-design/fundamentals/event-sourcing-and-cqrs), the
[queue](/system-design/fundamentals/message-queues-and-pubsub)'s
delivery, a click log), where "query the table" stops making sense
because the table never stops changing and never ends.

The reason the distinction is a *design* question rather than a
implementation detail: the two models answer different questions.
Batch answers "what happened, as of the data I have now"; streaming
answers "what is happening, continuously". And the entire
infrastructure of modern data platforms (Kafka, Flink, Spark) exists
because the honest answer for most real systems is *both*, with the
same events feeding both.

## Batch: the bounded world

A batch job takes a bounded input, processes it fully, and produces a
bounded output: the nightly reconciliation, the weekly report, the model
training run. Its virtues are *economic and semantic*: no latency
pressure (so the job can be cheap, restartable, retried), and
*completeness* (the job sees all of the data at once, so per-record
questions like dedup and exact ordering across the whole set are
trivially honest).

The costs are staleness (the freshest data is one cycle old) and the
coordination overhead of the cycle itself: the 2 AM window where
everything must finish before the business opens, which at scale
becomes its own failure domain (the famous "the nightly job did not
finish" outage, which is why batch systems at scale move to
incremental/checkpointed designs that are streaming wearing a batch
costume).

## Stream: the unbounded world

A streaming job consumes events *as they arrive* and maintains its
answer continuously: a running count, a rolling average, an alert when
the pattern shifts. The defining constraint is that **the job never
sees all the data**, so every question must be answerable from a
window of it, and the machinery exists to define which window.

**Windows** are how unbounded data gets bounded per-question: fixed
hourly buckets (tumbling), sliding last-4-hours (sliding), or
per-key session windows (grouped by activity gaps). The *aggregate*
becomes a function of the window, and the job is a pipeline of
per-window aggregations rather than a single sweep.

**Watermarks** are the honesty mechanism for *when a window can
close*: a stream is out of order by nature (a mobile event arriving on
reconnect, an [at-least-once](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once)
redelivery, a partition lagging), so "I have all the events for the
11:00 window" is a *claim* that must be licensed. A watermark is a
bound: "no event older than X will arrive after this point",
propagated through the pipeline, and the window closes when the
watermark passes it. Late events are then a policy: update (the
correct-but-expensive answer, reopening aggregates), or drop (the
event goes to a side output for handling), or ignore. The entire
correctness-versus-cost trade of streaming lives on that one dial.

**State** is what the window machinery implies: per-key, per-window
running aggregates, held by the job, checkpointed so a restart resumes
rather than restarts. That state is *also* unbounded (a million keys
times open windows), which is why stream processors (Flink, Kafka
Streams) are as much *state managers* as data movers: RocksDB-backed
state, changelogging, exactly-once commits.

## Exactly-once, priced honestly

The [idempotency page](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once)
establishes that exactly-once delivery does not exist; streaming
systems advertise "exactly-once semantics" anyway, and what they mean
is precise and narrow: *atomic commits of (stream offset, state
update)*, so a restarted job picks up exactly where it left off,
with no double-processing of the offset it was mid-way through.
Flink's two-phase checkpoint (barrier snapshot of all operators,
atomic with the Kafka offset) is the standard implementation, and it
is genuine engineering, *and* it expires at the boundary: the moment
the result goes to a non-transactional sink (a database, an HTTP
call), the job is back to at-least-once plus
[idempotent](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once)
writes, by the general theorem.

The honest cost model: exactly-once checkpoints cost *latency and
throughput* (every barrier flushes), at-least-once costs *dedup logic
at every sink*, and choosing per-pipeline is the actual decision.

## The architectures

**Lambda** (the classic): run *both* a batch pipeline (complete,
correct, stale) and a streaming pipeline (fresh, approximate) and
merge their outputs, with the batch layer periodically correcting the
stream's errors. Honest about the trade, expensive in practice: two
codebases to keep in agreement, forever.

**Kappa**: one codebase, the streaming one, with "reprocess from the
log" replacing the batch layer: the event store *is* the source of
truth, and a full recompute is replaying it with a different job. The
[architecture](/system-design/fundamentals/event-sourcing-and-cqrs)
underneath is the same one that page describes, and the reason Kappa
is the modern default: one set of bugs, one set of semantics, and the
"batch" is just the stream read with a bigger window.

**The honest streaming-replication of the batch world**: most modern
platforms (Kafka, with its log-retention-and-replay semantics) make
the *same events* feed both a streaming consumer (low latency) and a
batch consumer (huge window), so the choice is per-question, not
per-system.

## Choosing

- The answer must be fresh (seconds, minutes): **stream**.
- The answer needs the whole dataset (training, reconciliation,
  full-table joins): **batch**, or a stream replay with a giant window.
- The question is "alert when X" or "running aggregate": **stream**,
  with its window/watermark/state machinery.
- The question is "report as of yesterday": **batch**, and forcing it
  to a stream buys nothing but the machinery.

The default mistake is reaching for streaming for *freshness* without
pricing the machinery (state, watermarks, late events, exactly-once),
which is a large standing cost for a feature sometimes worth less than
a one-hour batch delay. The senior move is pricing the freshness
honestly, per question, before choosing.
