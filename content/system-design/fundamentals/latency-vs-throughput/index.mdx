---
title: "Latency vs. Throughput"
summary: "Two different meanings of 'fast', measured in different units, optimized by different moves, and traded against each other by every real system."
---

"Fast" is two claims wearing one word. **Latency** is how long one
request takes: 200 milliseconds, end to end, measured at the
percentile. **Throughput** is how many requests a system handles per
second: 10,000 of them, measured as a rate. The units differ (time per
request versus requests per time), and so does everything else: what
makes each one good, what makes each one bad, and who suffers when you
optimize one at the expense of the other.

The connection is real but loose: a system with low average latency
*usually* has high throughput, because the same efficiency drives
both. The divergences are the interesting part, and almost every
production performance decision is about one of them.

## The inverse that is not a law

If every request took exactly 10ms, latency and throughput would be
perfect inverses: 100 requests/sec per worker, full stop, nothing to
trade. Real traffic does not look like that: request times are
spread over orders of magnitude (a cache hit is 1ms, a cache miss with
a join is 500ms, a cold cache with a slow disk is 5s), and that spread
is where the two metrics come apart.

**Batching** is the canonical trade. A database that groups 100 writes
into one disk flush raises throughput enormously (the disk's cost is
per-flush, not per-byte) and raises the latency of the average write
(your write waits for the batch). A
[model server](/system-design/fundamentals/rate-limiting) batching
requests to a GPU doubles throughput and adds queueing delay to every
request in the batch. The [queue](/system-design/fundamentals/message-queues-and-pubsub)
itself is the same trade at architecture scale: buffer work, smooth the
pipeline, gain throughput, add waiting.

**Parallelism** is the other lever, and it helps both, with a ceiling:
adding workers lowers latency (less queueing) and raises throughput
until the bottleneck saturates, and then it does neither. The
[load balancer](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing) page's
scaling story is this curve.

## Percentiles: why the average lies

The single most important technical fact in this subject: **the average
latency is not the latency the user experiences**, because latency
distributions are heavy-tailed. A system with a 100ms mean can have a
99th percentile of 4 seconds (a minority of requests hitting GC pauses,
cache misses, lock contention, or an overloaded shard), and the users
whose requests land in that tail do not experience "a fast system with
some variance", they experience a system that is sometimes broken.

Hence the measurement discipline, from
[observability](/system-design/fundamentals/observability): report
**p50, p95, p99** (and sometimes p99.9), never the mean alone. The mean
answers "what is the total cost of all requests" (which is what
throughput cares about); the percentiles answer "what fraction of users
are having a bad time" (which is what the product cares about).

The tail's causes are worth memorizing, because they are the standard
checklist: **queueing** (the classic: at 80% utilization, queues grow
non-linearly; the [p99 is where queueing
lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queueing_theory)), **cache misses
and cold paths**, **GC and runtime pauses**, **lock contention**
(everyone waiting for one mutex), and **the overloaded neighbor**
(noisy-multitenant: one shard's hot user degrading everyone on that
box, which is the [multi-region](/system-design/fundamentals/multi-region-and-active-active)
tiering question in miniature).

## Tail latency and the fan-out tax

The tail gets worse when requests fan out, which is the modern
architecture's built-in trap: a page that calls 20 services takes the
*maximum* of their latencies, not the average, so 20 services each
99%-reliable at 100ms yield a page that is p99-broken by
multiplication. The [CAP page](/system-design/fundamentals/cap-theorem-and-consistency-models)
has the consistency version of this; the latency version is
"p99 * (number of hops) is your real p99", and it is the reason the
[microservices](/system-design/fundamentals/microservices-vs-monolith)
conversation keeps returning to depth.

## Choosing what to optimize

The product decides, per feature, which metric is real, and the
mismatch is the classic performance mistake:

- A **checkout flow** is latency-bound (a slow request is a lost sale;
  the [flash sale](/system-design/hld/e-commerce-flash-sale) page wants
  p99 under 500ms), and its throughput needs are modest for the same
  traffic.
- A **log pipeline or analytics job** is throughput-bound (nobody is
  waiting for *this one* log line), and its latency budget is measured
  in minutes.
- A **cache** is latency-bound by definition: its whole purpose is the
  1ms hit, which is why the
  [distributed cache](/system-design/hld/distributed-cache) pays a
  network hop rather than batching.
- A **feed builder** is throughput-bound ([fan-out](/system-design/hld/news-feed)
  is a write-amplification problem) with a latency budget of seconds,
  which is why the queue between "post created" and "feed visible"
  is acceptable by design.

The [SLO](/system-design/fundamentals/reliability-engineering)
discipline is the formal version: pick the metric per feature, pick
the percentile, put it in writing, and measure it. "Our p99 is under
500ms" is a promise that can be kept or broken, which is exactly why
it is more useful than "the system is fast".

## The queueing lens, briefly

The theory that prices all of this: an M/M/c queue (Poisson arrivals,
exponential service, c servers) says utilization ρ drives queue length
non-linearly, so at 50% utilization queues are negligible, at 80% they
are noticeable, at 95% they are the tail, and at 100% they are
unbounded. Little's Law (L = λ * W: things in system = arrival rate *
wait time) is the sanity check for everything: if 10,000 requests/sec
arrive and each spends 200ms in the system, 2,000 requests are in
flight *somewhere*, which sizes buffers, worker pools, and connection
limits, and is the single most useful equation for turning latency and
throughput numbers into each other.
