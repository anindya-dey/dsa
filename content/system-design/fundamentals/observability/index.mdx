---
title: "Observability"
summary: "Logs, metrics, and traces answer different questions, and a system is debuggable only when all three can be cross-referenced."
---

2 AM. An on-call engineer is paged: checkout latency is at the 99.9th
percentile. What do you reach for? That question is what observability
design is about: at the moment something breaks, you need enough
instrumentation, in the right shapes, to answer "what happened, where,
since when" without adding code to a production system.

The classic trio (logs, metrics, traces) are not three synonyms. They are
three different *questions*, with different storage, cost, and scale.

## Metrics: "how much, over time"

Metrics are numbers with timestamps: request rate, error count, p99
latency, CPU utilization, queue depth. They are aggregated by nature
(averages, histograms, counters), which makes them cheap to store, fast
to query, and easy to alert on. Alerting is the point: a dashboard
watching "error rate > 1% for 5 minutes" is what wakes the engineer at
2 AM, and it's metrics' job to power it.

The cost of that cheapness is *aggregation*: metrics tell you the checkout
service is slow, but not *which* request, for *which* user, or *why*. They
answer "how much, over time" and nothing else.

Two design decisions dominate metrics:

- **Percentiles over averages.** A mean latency of 150ms can be a
  beautifully fast system for 99% of users and an unusable timeout for
  1%. Latency distributions are heavy-tailed, and the tail is where the
  pain lives: alert on p95/p99, not the mean.
- **Cardinality.** Every distinct label combination (endpoint x region x
  status x ...) is a separate stored series. "Status code" is 50 series;
  "user ID" is 10 million series, and your metrics store dies. Cardinality
  discipline (aggregate away user IDs, keep the dimensions you'd actually
  debug with) is the difference between a metrics system that scales and
  one that falls over.

## Logs: "what exactly happened here"

Logs are the events: one line (or structured record) per thing that
happened, with whatever context you put in it. Unlike metrics, logs can
answer the *which* and *why*: this order, this user, this stack trace,
this error message.

The cost is volume. A service doing 10,000 requests/second that logs 10
lines per request is producing 100k lines/second, which must be shipped,
stored, and *retentioned* (typically 30 days hot, then cheap cold
storage, because nobody pays to keep debug logs from last year
queryable). That cost is also why "structured logging" (JSON records
with stable fields, not free-text strings) matters: fields are
queryable, strings are grep-able, and grep doesn't join.

## Traces: "where did the time go"

A trace follows one logical request across every service it touched: the
API call, the auth check, the two database reads, the queue publish,
the downstream service's internal work, each with its own timing. Spans
link into a tree, and the tree shows you the shape of the latency: 200ms
total, 180 of it in the inventory service, 170 of that in one database
query.

Traces are the only instrument that answers the distributed question
("*which* hop was slow?"), and their sampling is the design tension:
tracing every request is prohibitively expensive, so systems sample (1%
of requests, or 100% of erroring requests, or all requests slower than X
seconds). The sampling decision is what you're actually engineering:
tail-based sampling (keep the slow and failed traces) is more valuable
per dollar than head-based (keep 1% of everything), because the rare
traces are the ones you need.

## The whole: correlation

Any one of the three answers a narrow question. Debugging requires
moving between them:

- The **metric** (p99 latency spike) tells you *that* something is wrong.
- The **trace** (sampled from that window) tells you *where*.
- The **logs** (from that service, that time window) tell you *why*.

Making that movement mechanical is the actual engineering. The standard
is **context propagation**: every request carries a trace ID, services
pass it along, and every log line includes it. Now "p99 spike at 02:13"
becomes "show me traces from 02:13" becomes "show me the logs for trace
ID abc123", and the 2 AM investigation is ten minutes instead of an hour.
Without a shared identifier, each instrument is a separate, unjoinable
island, and you're back to correlating by timestamps and hope.

## The SLO connection

Observability's output is not dashboards, it's *decisions*, and the
frame that turns measurement into decisions is
[reliability engineering](/system-design/fundamentals/reliability-engineering):
define an SLO (99.9% of checkout requests under 500ms), measure it with
metrics, and spend the error budget on deliberate risk. Under that
frame, alerts become "the SLO is burning" instead of "some threshold was
crossed", which is the difference between alerts that get acted on and
alerts that get muted.
