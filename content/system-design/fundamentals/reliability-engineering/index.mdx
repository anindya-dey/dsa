---
title: "Reliability Engineering (SLOs & Error Budgets)"
summary: "Deciding, in advance, how much unreliability is acceptable, then spending that budget like the resource it is."
---

A system that is "reliable" is a system whose users cannot tell, most of
the time, that anything is wrong. That sentence, from the SRE tradition,
is deliberately softer than "the system is up", because *up* is not a
binary the users experience: they experience "my request worked, fast
enough, correctly". Reliability engineering is the practice of making
that experience **explicit, measured, and budgeted**: define what
good looks like, measure it continuously, and treat the gap between
perfection and your promise as a spendable resource.

The reason this discipline exists rather than "just make it reliable":
perfection is arbitrarily expensive. 99.9% availability is roughly 8.8
hours of downtime a year, 99.99% is 52 minutes, and each extra nine
costs more than the last, in engineering effort and in *velocity*
(because the usual way to be more reliable is to change less often).
The core insight is that unreliability is not a failure to eliminate
but a *budget to allocate*.

## SLI, SLO, SLA: the three words

**SLI** (service level indicator) is the measurement: a ratio of good
events to total events. Not "uptime" but "fraction of requests that
succeeded", "fraction that answered under 400ms", "fraction of days
the nightly job finished by 9am". The art of SLI design is picking
indicators that track the *user's* experience rather than the server's
internals: a request that returned 200 in 5 seconds because a cache
served a stale page did not have a good experience, and "requests
succeeded" alone will not say so.

**SLO** (service level objective) is the promise *to yourself*: the SLI
target, over a window. "99.9% of requests succeed, measured monthly."
It is an internal target, and it is deliberately *below* 100%.

**SLA** (service level agreement) is the promise to a *customer*, with
consequences (contractual, financial) attached. It is always looser than
your internal SLO, because you must have room to have a bad month
without breaching the contract: the SLA is the floor, the SLO is where
you actually aim.

The relationship worth internalizing: SLI is the *measurement*, SLO is
the *internal target over that measurement*, SLA is the *external floor
under it*. And the most common anti-pattern is picking 99.99% for
everything: it is either an unexamined default ("more nines must be
better") or a promise the product never needed, and either way it
silently spends the budget on nothing.

## The error budget: the resource view

The complement of the SLO is the **error budget**: 100% minus the
target, and therefore the amount of unreliability you are *allowed*
each window. At 99.9%, the budget is 43 minutes of full outage (or
0.1% of all requests failing, mixed however they come) per month.

The budget is a *resource* because of what spending it buys, and the
spenders are the interesting part:

- **Changes**: deploys, migrations, and rollouts each spend budget (the
  bugs they carry). When the budget is spent, the *release train
  slows*: freeze features, ship only fixes. This is the practice that
  makes the budget real, and it is the one that generates organizational
  friction, because "the budget is spent, we ship no features this
  sprint" is a conversation between engineering and product that the
  budget *settles by policy rather than argument*.
- **Planned maintenance**: a maintenance window spends budget like an
  outage does (users cannot tell the difference), which is the honest
  accounting that pushes systems toward zero-downtime deploys.
- **Attacks of reality**: the disk dies, the region flakes, the
  dependency has a bad day. You cannot schedule these, which is why the
  *leftover* budget after planned spend is the buffer.

The budget *burn rate* is how you know you are in trouble early:
dividing the budget by the window (a 43-minute monthly budget burns at
about 1 minute per 16 hours) gives you thresholds, and "we burned 10% of
the monthly budget in an hour" is a *page*, not a footnote. That is the
alerting discipline: alerts on budget burn rather than on individual
incidents, because a hundred small failures and one big one spend the
same budget, and the user cannot tell which occurred.

## What the user actually experiences: the SLI menu

The honest SLI set for a request-serving system, and the standard
complaints each one answers:

- **Availability**: fraction of requests that did not error. Complaint
  it prevents: "it was down".
- **Latency**: fraction under a threshold (and *measured at the
  tail*, [p99](/system-design/fundamentals/observability), not the
  mean). Complaint: "it was so slow I gave up".
- **Correctness / freshness**: fraction of responses that were right
  and current. Complaint: "it worked but showed stale/wrong data", which
  no uptime metric catches, and which is where
  [eventual consistency](/system-design/fundamentals/cap-theorem-and-consistency-models)
  becomes a *user-visible* reliability question.
- **Throughput**: fraction of time the system absorbed offered load.
  Complaint: "during the sale it fell over."

Choosing which SLIs *matter* is the product conversation: a checkout flow
needs availability *and* correctness; a recommendation box tolerates
stale and slow happily. The per-feature SLO (strict where money moves,
loose where a filler panel loads) is the mature output, and it is the
same per-feature consistency assignment as every other page in this
catalog makes.

## The operational loop

1. **Define** SLIs and SLOs per service, from the user experience
   backward, with the window and the measurement method stated.
2. **Measure** continuously ([observability](/system-design/fundamentals/observability):
   the SLI must be computable from the telemetry, or it is a wish).
3. **Alert on burn**, not on raw thresholds: fast-burn pages, slow-burn
   tickets.
4. **Account for spending**: deploys and maintenance consume budget on
   the record, and budget-exhausted triggers the release freeze.
5. **Review** after each window: what spent the budget, what the
   customer saw, and whether the target should move. The error budget
   is also the *argument-settler* for "should we invest in reliability
   or ship features": when the budget is chronically underspent, the
   honest answer is features; chronically overspent, reliability.
