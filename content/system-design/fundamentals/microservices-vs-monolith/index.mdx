---
title: "Microservices vs. Monolith"
summary: "One deployable or many: the real trade is organizational, and the technical costs follow it in both directions."
---

The question "microservices or a monolith?" is usually asked as a
technical question and is almost always an *organizational* one. The
technology of both shapes is well understood, and the difference in
outcomes comes overwhelmingly from a different source: **who can
deploy what, how independently, at what coordination cost**. A monolith
is one deployable unit where every team ships together; microservices
are many units where each team ships alone. Everything else, the
scaling stories, the failure isolation, the polyglot freedom, and
also the distributed-systems tax and the operational burden, is a
consequence of that choice, and deciding well means pricing the
consequences in that order.

## What each shape actually is

A **monolith** is one deployable: one process (or a few identical
ones behind a
[load balancer](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing)), one
codebase, one database, one release. Its virtues are underrated and
mostly forgotten by people who only hear the counterargument:

- **One transaction boundary**: money moves across modules in a single
  ACID transaction, and the
  [consistency](/system-design/fundamentals/distributed-transactions)
  machinery is the database's, mature and boring.
- **One deployment artifact**: no version skew between modules, no
  [contract evolution](/system-design/fundamentals/serialization-and-schema-evolution)
  problem, no "service B is on an old API and we cannot deploy C".
- **In-process calls**: a function call, nanoseconds, no
  serialization, no retry semantics, no partial failure.

**Microservices** split the system into independently deployable
services, each owning its data, communicating over the network. The
split's *purpose* is independence: teams deploy without coordinating,
scale and fail independently, and own their domains end to end. The
technical claims (scaling parts separately, isolating failures,
polyglot languages) are real but secondary: they are bonuses, and the
org-level independence is the product.

## The honest trade ledger

What you *buy* with microservices, and what you *pay*, stated so the
decision can be made rather than assumed:

**Bought:**

- **Independent deployment**: the headline. Teams ship their own
  cadence, and a slow team does not block the fleet.
- **Independent scaling**: the
  [hot tier](/system-design/fundamentals/scalability) scales without
  paying for the rest.
- **Failure isolation**: one service's crash is a degraded feature, not
  a dead product (if the callers degrade gracefully, which is work).
- **Clear ownership boundaries**: Conway's law in the good direction,
  with a team per domain.

**Paid:**

- **The distributed-systems tax**: every internal call is now a
  network call, which means latency, partial failure, retries,
  [idempotency](/system-design/fundamentals/idempotency-and-exactly-once),
  timeouts, and circuit breakers, on every path, forever.
  [CAP](/system-design/fundamentals/cap-theorem-and-consistency-models)
  stops being a slide and starts being your Tuesday.
- **Cross-service consistency**: the single transaction is gone, and
  "transfer money between two services' accounts" becomes the
  [saga/outbox](/system-design/fundamentals/distributed-transactions)
  machinery, the hardest tax in this catalog.
- **Operational surface area**: dozens of deployables, each needing
  observability, deployment pipelines, secrets, and
  [an internal proxy layer](/system-design/fundamentals/api-gateway-and-service-mesh)
  before any of it is manageable.
- **Contract discipline**: the
  [schema evolution](/system-design/fundamentals/serialization-and-schema-evolution)
  rules become load-bearing, because services are deployed at
  different versions, at different times, permanently.

The summary that should be recited before choosing: **a monolith
trades deployment independence for simplicity; microservices trade
simplicity for deployment independence**, and the correct choice
depends almost entirely on how many teams you have and how much they
need to ship independently. The famous heuristic: *if you cannot run
a monolith well, you cannot run microservices either* (the operational
maturity the monolith demands is a subset of what microservices
demand), and the second one: the split is not irreversible in either
direction, but "merge back" is easier said than done.

## The decomposition discipline (when you do split)

The failure mode of microservices is not the technology, it is the
*boundaries*: a split along the wrong lines buys all the costs and
none of the independence. The disciplines worth reciting:

- **Split by business capability, not by technical layer.** "Orders",
  "Payments", "Inventory", not "a data layer" (which couples every
  feature to one deploy), not "a service per table" (which
  distributes the database without distributing anything worth
  distributing).
- **Own your data.** A service's database is private, accessed only
  through its API; the moment another service reads the table, the
  boundary is a lie and the coupling is worse than the monolith's
  (it is distributed coupling, the worst of both).
- **Design for the failure of every dependency.** Each caller
  degrades gracefully, caches, queues, or fails loudly, because the
  dependency *will* be down
  ([backpressure](/system-design/fundamentals/backpressure-and-flow-control)
  is the caller's problem, not the callee's).
- **The [strangler fig](/system-design/hld/large-scale-migration)**
  is the honest migration path: extract a service when its boundary
  is clear and its independence pays, incrementally, never a rewrite.

## The modern middle grounds

The binary is false in both directions, and the current practice
names the in-betweens:

- **Modular monolith**: one deployable, strictly module boundaries,
  private data per module, enforced by build rules. Gives you the
  *boundaries* (and a future extraction path) without the distributed
  tax. The answer for most teams under ~50 engineers, and the honest
  starting point for everyone.
- **Service-oriented with a few coarse services**: split at the
  *seams that already hurt* (the scaling hotspot, the deploy-frequency
  hotspot), not everywhere. The
  [estimation](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
  discipline applied to architecture: split where the numbers say to
  split.
- **Eventual consistency as a design tool**: once split, lean on
  [event-driven](/system-design/fundamentals/event-sourcing-and-cqrs)
  integration rather than synchronous chains, because a call chain of
  five microservices is a latency and availability story that a queue
  makes boring.
