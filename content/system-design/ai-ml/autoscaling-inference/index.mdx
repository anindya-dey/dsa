---
title: "Autoscaling for Inference"
summary: "Scaling a serving fleet on the right signal, when the unit of work is a slow, expensive request."
---

Autoscaling a model-serving fleet is the [load
balancer](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing) scaling problem
with three ML-specific twists that break the standard signals: the
**unit of work is slow** (a forward pass is milliseconds-to-seconds,
so in-flight requests pile up), the **resource is
special** ([GPU](/system-design/ai-ml/gpu-accelerator-management):
scaling out means *acquiring a scarce device*, not starting a cheap
process), and the **warm-up is long** (a new replica takes
seconds-to-minutes to be *ready*: the
[cold-start](/system-design/ai-ml/serverless-inference) problem at
fleet scale). The design: **scale on the signal that predicts the
bottleneck** (queue depth, not CPU), with the *lead time* the warm-up
demands.

## Why the standard signals fail

- **CPU utilization**: a GPU-backed model can be *CPU-idle* (the CPU
  waits for the GPU) while saturated, or CPU-busy on preprocessing
  while the GPU is idle — CPU does not track the bottleneck, so
  scaling on it under- or over-provisions unpredictably.
- **Memory**: a model's memory is *fixed* (the weights), so memory
  utilization is a *binary* (loaded or not), not a load signal.
- **Request rate alone**: without knowing the *service time*
  (which varies with batch size and input size), rate does not imply
  load — the same RPS is trivial or fatal depending on the model
  and the inputs.
- **The lagging signal**: by the time CPU is high, the queue is
  already deep and the p99 is already breached (the
  [queueing-theory](/system-design/fundamentals/latency-vs-throughput)
  lesson: the wait explodes near saturation, so reacting *at*
  saturation is too late).

## The right signal: queue depth (or its proxies)

The signal that *predicts* the bottleneck is the **queue**: the
requests waiting to be served. It leads utilization (it rises
*before* the workers are saturated), it directly encodes the user
experience (queue depth × service time ≈ wait), and it is the
standard autoscaling signal for slow-workload fleets
(the same reason the [job scheduler](/system-design/hld/distributed-job-scheduler)
scales on queue depth). Proxies where a true queue is not visible:
**in-flight request count** (the requests currently being served:
rises as concurrency exceeds capacity), **p99 latency** (the
[latency-SLO](/system-design/fundamentals/latency-vs-throughput)
as the signal: scale when the tail breaches, the
[reliability](/system-design/fundamentals/reliability-engineering)
framing), and the **batching queue length** (the
[dynamic-batching](/system-design/ai-ml/batching-and-latency)
tier's own backlog: the most direct ML-specific signal).

## The lead time: scaling ahead of the warm-up

The twist that dominates the design: **a new replica is not ready
when it starts** — it must schedule, provision, load the model, and
warm up (seconds to minutes). So autoscaling must **lead the load**,
not follow it, which means:

- **Predictive / proactive scaling** (scale on the *forecast*
  (daily patterns, scheduled events): the
  [capacity-planning](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
  discipline, because reacting to a spike with a 2-minute warm-up
  means 2 minutes of degraded service — the honest admission that
  *reactive* autoscaling cannot cover a *fast* spike for a *slow*
  workload, and the pre-warm is the answer).
- **Over-provisioned headroom** (keep a margin above the predicted
  load, sized by the warm-up time: the faster the spike and the
  slower the warm-up, the bigger the floor — a
  [capacity-planning](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
  number, and the reason a serving fleet runs "hot").
- **Scale-in is *slow and careful*** (do not drop a replica that is
  mid-request, and do not drop the last warm copy of a model: the
  [drain](/system-design/fundamentals/load-balancing) discipline,
  with the model-warmth constraint added — scaling in *below* the
  [warm pool's](/system-design/ai-ml/model-caching-warm-pools)
  needs is the self-inflicted cold start).

## The scale-to-zero tension

The [serverless](/system-design/ai-ml/serverless-inference) ideal
(scale to zero) fights the warm-up reality, and the design resolves
it by **workload class**:

- **Interactive, high-traffic**: scale on queue depth, keep a warm
  floor, scale out ahead of the forecast — *never* to zero (the
  floor is the price of the latency SLO).
- **Long-tail, low-traffic**: scale to zero (or to the
  [warm-pool floor](/system-design/ai-ml/model-caching-warm-pools)),
  accept the cold start for the rare request, and cover it with the
  [single-flight/queueing](/system-design/ai-ml/serverless-inference)
  trick.
- **Bursty**: the hybrid — a warm floor sized to the *baseline*, fast
  scale-out for the burst, and the [autoscaling](/system-design/ai-ml/autoscaling-inference)
  policy tuned to the *ramp*, not the peak.

## The capacity-planning arithmetic

The numbers the policy needs: **service time per request** (the
model's latency, batch-dependent), **concurrency per replica**
(how many in-flight requests one GPU sustains: the batching and
[GPU](/system-design/ai-ml/gpu-accelerator-management) limits), and
**warm-up time** (the lead time). From those: *replicas needed =
peak RPS × service time / concurrency* (Little's Law, the
[latency page's](/system-design/fundamentals/latency-vs-throughput)
tool), and *the headroom = the load during the warm-up window* — the
two numbers that make "how many replicas, and how far ahead" a
computed answer rather than a guess.
