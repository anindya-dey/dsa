---
title: "The Curse of Dimensionality"
summary: "Why high-dimensional space behaves strangely, and what it means for models."
---

Machine learning lives in *high-dimensional* space: a model with a
thousand features operates in a thousand-dimensional space, and that
space does not behave like the two or three dimensions human intuition
is built for. The **curse of dimensionality** is the collection of
ways high-dimensional space is *counterintuitive*, and it explains
several ML behaviors that otherwise seem mysterious (why more
features can hurt, why nearest-neighbor methods degrade, why
dimensionality reduction helps).

## The strange geometry

The surprises, each with a consequence:

- **Volume concentrates at the edges** (in a high-dimensional cube,
  almost all the volume is near the surface, and almost none near the
  center, a ball inscribed in the cube occupies a vanishing fraction
  of it. The consequence: "the middle" is nearly empty, so data
  points are *all* on the periphery).
- **Everything is roughly equidistant** (as dimensions grow, the
  distances between random points *converge*, the farthest point and
  the nearest point become almost the same distance away. The
  consequence: "nearest neighbor" loses meaning, which is why
  [k-NN](/ai-ml/concepts/clustering-and-knn) and raw
  [similarity search](/ai-ml/systems/similarity-search-ann) struggle
  in very high dimensions, and why [embeddings](/ai-ml/systems/embeddings-vector-representations)
  use *learned* spaces where the geometry is meaningful).
- **You need exponentially more data** (to cover a space at a fixed
  density, the required examples grow *exponentially* with the
  dimensions. The consequence: with a thousand features, no realistic
  dataset densely covers the space, so models *must* make assumptions
  (inductive biases) to generalize: they cannot just interpolate
  between seen examples).

## What it means for models

- **More features is not more information** (adding irrelevant
  features *hurts*: the model has more dimensions to overfit in, the
  [bias-variance](/ai-ml/concepts/overfitting-and-bias-variance)
  trade worsens, and distance-based methods degrade. This is why
  *feature selection* and *dimensionality reduction* are core tools,
  not luxuries).
- **Dimensionality reduction** (techniques like
  PCA
  and autoencoders project high-dimensional data to a lower
  dimension, keeping the structure and discarding noise, the
  [autoencoder](/ai-ml/concepts/autoencoders) idea, and a standard
  preprocessing step).
- **The manifold hypothesis** (the modern saving grace: real-world
  high-dimensional data is not *uniformly* spread, it lies on a
  *low-dimensional structure* (a manifold) embedded in the high
  dimension. Images have millions of pixels but far fewer *degrees of
  freedom*; language has vast vocabularies but lower intrinsic
  structure. Deep learning works *because* it learns that structure,
which is why [embeddings](/ai-ml/concepts/word-embeddings) can
  compress meaning into a few hundred dimensions, and why the
  curse is *tamed* rather than *suffered* in practice).

## Why it is worth knowing

The curse explains a set of practical behaviors: why nearest-neighbor
search needs [approximate indexes](/ai-ml/systems/similarity-search-ann)
in high dimensions, why adding features without adding data can
*worsen* a model, why dimensionality reduction helps, and why deep
learning's learned representations are so valuable (they *find* the
low-dimensional structure that makes the data tractable). It is the
geometric intuition behind a lot of ML practice.
