---
title: "Overfitting, Underfitting & Bias-Variance"
summary: "Memorizing vs. generalizing, and the trade-off at the heart of model choice."
---

The central tension of machine learning: a model must do well on data
it has *never seen*, but it only *learns* from data it *has* seen.
Get the balance wrong in one direction and the model **memorizes**
the training examples without learning the pattern
(**overfitting**); wrong in the other and it is too simple to capture
the pattern at all (**underfitting**). The **bias-variance tradeoff**
is the framework for that balance, and it is the single most useful
mental model for diagnosing why a model is not working.

## The two failure modes

- **Underfitting** (high bias): the model is too simple, a straight
  line through a curve, or too few parameters. It does *badly on the
  training data too*, because it cannot represent the pattern. The
  symptom: training error is high. The fix: a more expressive model,
  better features, or less regularization.
- **Overfitting** (high variance): the model is too flexible, it
  fits the *noise* in the training data, not just the signal. It does
  *great on training data and poorly on new data*. The symptom: a
  large gap between training and validation error. The fix: more
  data, regularization, a simpler model, or early stopping.

The diagnostic, stated once: **look at both training error and
validation error.** High training error → underfitting. Low training
but high validation → overfitting. Both low → good. Both high with a
gap → overfitting that also needs more capacity.

## The bias-variance decomposition (the framework)

The expected error of a model decomposes into three parts:

- **Bias** (error from wrong assumptions: a too-simple model is
  *biased* toward simple answers, and misses the pattern).
- **Variance** (error from sensitivity to the training data: a
  too-flexible model *varies* wildly with which examples it saw).
- **Irreducible error** (noise in the data itself: no model can
  beat it, the floor).

The **tradeoff**: increasing model complexity *lowers bias and raises
variance*; decreasing it does the opposite. There is a sweet spot in
between, and finding it, via validation, regularization, and data
size, is the core of model selection. The picture is a U-shaped
total-error curve: high at both extremes (underfit on the left,
overfit on the right), lowest in the middle.

## What moves each side

**Toward overfitting** (more variance): a more complex model, more
features, less data, training too long, no regularization. **Toward
underfitting** (more bias): a simpler model, fewer features, too much
regularization, training too little.

The levers that reduce variance *without* adding bias, the most
valuable ones: **more data** (the single best fix: variance shrinks
as the model sees more examples), **regularization** (the
[penalty](/ai-ml/concepts/regularization) that constrains
complexity), **ensembling** (averaging many models cancels their
independent errors, the
[random-forest](/ai-ml/concepts/decision-trees-and-ensembles) idea),
and **early stopping** (stop before the model starts fitting noise).

## Why it is the master diagnostic

Almost every "the model does not work" conversation reduces to this
framework: is it *underfitting* (needs more capacity) or *overfitting*
(needs more data or constraint)? The reason it is so useful is that
the *symptom* (the train/validation gap) *names the fix*. And it is
why the [train/validation/test split](/ai-ml/concepts/train-validation-test)
exists at all: you cannot see overfitting without data the model was
not trained on.

The production echo: an overfit model *passes the offline
evaluation* and *fails in production*, the
[offline/online gap](/ai-ml/systems/offline-vs-online-evaluation), and
the reason generalization, not training accuracy, is the thing to
measure.
