Gradient boosting is the model that wins most tabular-data
competitions and powers a huge fraction of real-world prediction
(ranking, fraud, risk, recommendations). It is a
[decision-tree](/ai-ml/concepts/decision-trees-and-ensembles) ensemble
like the random forest, but built *sequentially* instead of in
parallel — and that single change (each tree *corrects* the previous
ones) is why it is usually more accurate.

## The idea: learn from mistakes, repeatedly

Where a random forest trains many independent trees and averages
them, gradient boosting trains trees *one at a time*, each fitted to
the **residual errors** of the ensemble so far:

1. Start with a trivial prediction (say, the average).
2. Compute the errors (the [loss](/ai-ml/concepts/loss-functions-and-risk)
   gradient) for every example.
3. Train a small tree to *predict those errors*.
4. Add it to the ensemble (scaled by a small *learning rate*), so the
   combined prediction is a little less wrong.
5. Repeat hundreds or thousands of times.

Each tree is *weak* (shallow — a few splits), and the ensemble is
*strong*: the sequence of corrections drives the loss down, and the
[learning rate](/ai-ml/concepts/gradient-descent) controls how much
each tree contributes (small rate = slower but more accurate, the
standard trade). It is, literally, **gradient descent in the space of
functions** — each tree is a step, and "boosting" is the name for
building a strong model from weak ones.

## Why it beats a random forest (usually)

- **Sequential correction** focuses each new tree on the examples the
  ensemble is still getting wrong, whereas bagging treats every tree
  equally and hopes averaging suffices.
- **A flexible loss** (gradient boosting is defined by the loss's
  gradient, so it works with *any* differentiable loss —
  classification, ranking, custom business losses — the reason it
  dominates ranking and risk models).
- **Strong regularization built in** (shallow trees, a small learning
  rate, row/feature subsampling, and early stopping — the modern
  implementations (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) are *regularization
  engineering* as much as boosting).

The cost: it is **harder to train** than a random forest (more
hyperparameters, more sensitive — a too-high learning rate or too
many trees [overfits](/ai-ml/concepts/overfitting-and-bias-variance)),
and it is *sequential* (less parallelizable than bagging, though
modern implementations parallelize within and across trees).

## The families worth naming

- **XGBoost / LightGBM / CatBoost**: the production implementations,
  each an engineering advance (LightGBM's histogram splits and leaf
  growth for speed; CatBoost's native categorical handling; XGBoost's
  ubiquity). Knowing they exist and roughly why is enough; the
  concept is the boosting loop.
- **Feature importances and SHAP** (which features the ensemble
  relies on, and per-prediction attributions): the
  [interpretability](/ai-ml/concepts/interpretability) surface, and
  a real reason the family is used in regulated settings despite
  being an ensemble.

## The verdict

Gradient boosting is the **default strong model for tabular data**:
almost always competitive, often the winner, and hard to beat with a
neural network on tables. It is also the model behind a lot of
[CTR/ranking](/ai-ml/systems/ctr-ad-ranking) and
[fraud detection](/system-design/hld/fraud-detection) systems. If you learn one
classical model deeply, learn this family — it is the one that
actually ships.
