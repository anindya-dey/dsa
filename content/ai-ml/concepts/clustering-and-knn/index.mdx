Two of the most intuitive ideas in machine learning, and both built
on **similarity**: **clustering** groups data points that are close
together (unsupervised — no labels), and **k-nearest neighbors
(k-NN)** predicts a point's label from the labels of its nearest
neighbors (supervised). They are simple enough to explain to a
non-engineer, and they teach the core notion of *distance as
meaning* that underlies embeddings, retrieval, and vector search.

## Clustering: finding groups without labels

The task: given unlabeled data, find natural groups. The canonical
algorithm, **k-means**:

1. Choose `k` cluster centers (randomly, or smartly).
2. Assign each point to its nearest center.
3. Move each center to the average of its assigned points.
4. Repeat until the centers stop moving.

It is the [expectation-maximization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation%E2%80%93maximization_algorithm)
idea in its simplest form — alternate "assign" and "update" — and it
converges to a local optimum. The decisions: **how many clusters**
(`k`, chosen by inspecting the data or a heuristic like the "elbow"
method), **the distance metric** (Euclidean usually), and the
limitations (**k-means assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized
clusters** and is sensitive to initialization — the reason
[k-means++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-means%2B%2B) exists).
Other families (hierarchical, density-based (DBSCAN), spectral)
handle shapes k-means cannot.

Where clustering is used: customer segmentation, document grouping,
[anomaly detection](/ai-ml/systems/anomaly-detection-system) (points
far from any cluster are suspicious), and as a *feature* (cluster id
as an input to a downstream model). The honest caveat: clusters are
*descriptive*, not *predictive* — there is no "right" answer, only
useful ones, which is why clustering is
[evaluated subjectively](/ai-ml/systems/supervised-unsupervised-rl)
and pairs badly with a single metric.

## k-Nearest Neighbors: predict from the neighbors

The simplest supervised model: to predict a new point's label, find
its `k` nearest training points and take a majority vote (or average,
for regression). There is **no training** — the model *is* the
training data, and prediction is a lookup. That "lazy learning"
property is both its charm (dead simple, no fitting) and its cost
(prediction is expensive: you compare against every training point,
and you store all of them).

The decisions: **k** (small = sensitive to noise, large = overly
smooth — a [bias-variance](/ai-ml/concepts/overfitting-and-bias-variance)
knob), **the distance metric** (the notion of "near" *is* the model),
and **weighting** (closer neighbors count more). Its strengths: it
captures arbitrarily complex boundaries and needs no assumptions about
the data's shape. Its weaknesses: **slow at prediction time**
(unless you index it — the
[ANN](/ai-ml/systems/similarity-search-ann) machinery, which is k-NN's
scalable form), **sensitive to irrelevant features and scale** (which
is why feature scaling is mandatory), and it degrades in high
dimensions (the [curse of dimensionality](/ai-ml/concepts/curse-of-dimensionality)).

## The shared foundation: similarity as meaning

Both models rest on a **distance metric**, and that is the deep
lesson: *choosing what "close" means is choosing what the model
considers similar* — the same idea that makes
[embeddings](/ai-ml/concepts/word-embeddings) and
[vector search](/ai-ml/systems/semantic-vector-search) work. A
k-NN's metric, a clustering's metric, and an embedding's geometry are
the same concept: **the space encodes the meaning**, and getting the
space right (via features or a learned embedding) matters more than
the algorithm.
