---
title: "Language Models & Scaling Laws"
summary: "Predict the next token, scale it up, and capabilities emerge."
---

A language model is a model that assigns probabilities to text, most
usefully, **the probability of the next token given the previous
ones**. Train a large [transformer](/ai-ml/concepts/transformers) on
that single objective (predict the next word) over a huge amount of
text, scale it up, and you get the systems behind modern AI: chatbots,
code assistants, translators, summarizers. The story of the last
several years is, in large part, the story of one simple objective
plus enormous scale producing surprisingly general capabilities.

## The objective: next-token prediction

The task sounds trivial: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next
one. Trained over the whole internet's worth of text, it forces the
model to learn grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and style, because
predicting the next token *well* requires modeling all of that. This
is **self-supervised** learning: no labels are needed, because the
text provides its own targets (the next token is always known). That
is what makes training on trillions of tokens possible.

Generation is **autoregressive**: predict a token, append it, predict
the next, repeat, the same loop as
[sequence-to-sequence](/ai-ml/concepts/sequence-to-sequence), scaled
up. A "chatbot" is this loop with the conversation as context.

## Scaling laws

The empirical discovery that drove everything: **model quality
improves predictably as you increase model size, data, and compute**,
a smooth power-law relationship, not a lucky accident. This let labs
*plan* training runs (choose a compute budget, predict the resulting
loss) and justified spending enormous resources on ever-larger models.
A key refinement: size and data must grow *together* (a huge model
trained on too little data is wasteful, and vice versa), and there is
a compute-optimal balance between them.

## Emergent abilities

A striking and debated phenomenon: as models scale, capabilities
appear that were *not* present (or not explicitly trained) at smaller
sizes: arithmetic, translation, multi-step reasoning, in-context
learning (learning a task from examples *in the prompt*, with no
weight updates). These **emergent abilities** are the reason scaling
generated so much excitement, and the reason a general-purpose model
can do tasks no one specifically trained it for. (How sharp and
predictable emergence really is remains an active research debate.)

## From base model to assistant

A raw next-token model (a "base model") is a text *completer*, not an
assistant: ask it a question and it might continue with more
questions, because that is what the training data looks like. Turning
it into a helpful assistant takes post-training:
[instruction tuning](/ai-ml/concepts/transfer-learning) (fine-tune on
instruction-following examples) and
[RLHF](/ai-ml/concepts/rlhf) (align outputs to human preferences).
This is why "the model" is really a *base model plus a post-training
recipe*, and why the same base model can be tuned into very different
assistants.

## The systems implications

Scaling a language model is a systems problem as much as an
algorithmic one: **distributed training** across thousands of
accelerators (data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism:
[distributed training](/ai-ml/systems/distributed-training)), efficient
**serving** of a model too large for one GPU with
[batching and KV-cache management](/ai-ml/systems/model-serving-architectures), and
the memory/compute tradeoffs of
[Mixture-of-Experts](/ai-ml/concepts/mixture-of-experts). The
concepts and the infrastructure are inseparable at this scale.
