title: "Time-Series Forecasting"
summary: "Predicting the next value from its own history: trend, seasonality, and why the naive baseline is hard to beat."

A time series is any measurement taken in order over time: hourly
traffic, daily sales, a stock price every second. Forecasting asks the
narrow, brutal question: what will the *next* values be, given only the
past ones? It is the rare ML setting where time is the whole game,
because the past is all you have and the future refuses to hold still
while you look at it.

The field's most important fact, learned by everyone who tries it: **the
naive baseline is hard to beat**. "Tomorrow equals today" (or, with
weekly patterns, "next Tuesday equals this Tuesday") is a forecast with
zero parameters, and sophisticated models regularly lose to it. Any
model you build has to justify its existence *against* that baseline,
and most don't.

## The vocabulary: what a series is made of

Most series decompose into three behaviors:

- **Trend**: the long drift, up or down.
- **Seasonality**: repeating patterns with a fixed period (daily, weekly,
  yearly). Traffic peaks every morning; retail spikes every December.
- **Remainder**: the noise left over, hopefully random.

A forecast is a bet that these components *persist*. Trend and
seasonality usually do, for a while, and that persistence is exactly
what a model exploits. When it stops persisting, the series has
**changed regime**, and every method here fails at once, which is the
moment this becomes less a modeling problem and more a
[drift](/ai-ml/systems/concept-drift-retraining) problem.

## The classical toolkit

**Exponential smoothing**, and its grown-up version Holt-Winters, is
statistics for a series: an exponentially weighted moving average that
*is* the model, extended with explicit trend and seasonality terms.
Interpretable, cheap, and strong on stable series. If you have nothing
but a few years of history, start here.

**ARIMA** is the same idea through autoregression: predict the next
value from a weighted mix of recent values (the AR part) and recent
*errors* (the MA part), after differencing away the trend. Fitting the
orders is its own art. It deserves respect for one structural insight:
it forces you to decide whether your series is *stationary* (its
statistics don't drift), because nothing works honestly on a series
that keeps changing what it is.

**Gradient boosting on lag features** turns forecasting into ordinary
supervised learning: the features are lags, rolling means, day-of-week,
holiday flags, and a tree ensemble
[regresses](/ai-ml/concepts/gradient-boosting) the next value off them.
This is the default winner on messy real-world series with external
signals (promotions, weather), because trees absorb calendar effects
and interactions without you specifying the seasonality by hand.

**Neural sequence models** (deep
[recurrent](/ai-ml/concepts/recurrent-neural-networks) or transformer
forecasts) are the frontier option: strongest when you have *many
related* series to learn from together (every store, every SKU), where
the network learns the shared seasonality and borrows strength across
series, or when the series is very long and high-dimensional. On a
single short series, they overfit and lose to smoothing.

## The evaluation traps

Forecasting's evaluation is where good models go to die.

**You cannot split randomly.** A random
[train/validation split](/ai-ml/concepts/train-validation-test) leaks
the future into the training set: the model trains on October to predict
September, and looks brilliant, and ships broken. Splits must be *in
time*: train on the past, validate on the future, and use
walk-forward validation (train to month k, predict month k+1, advance k,
repeat) so the evaluation matches deployment.

**The horizon is a choice, and per-horizon skill differs.** A model
may be excellent at tomorrow and hopeless at next month, because each
step ahead compounds error. Score each horizon separately, and be
suspicious of any single-number summary.

**Metrics punish asymmetry differently.** MAE treats every miss
proportionally; squared-error metrics punish big misses; MAPE
blows up on small actual values. A forecast that is "right most days,
catastrophically wrong on holidays" looks great under one metric and
gets you paged under another.

## The honest failure mode

Every classical method assumes the *process* keeps behaving. Breaks in
the series (a product launch, a pandemic, a silent tracking change)
invalidate the past, and no amount of smoothing recovers. The mature
response is operational: monitor the residuals like a
[monitoring](/ai-ml/systems/model-monitoring-drift) system watches
inputs, detect when the errors stop looking like the training errors,
and plan to re-fit on shorter windows, because in a drifting world,
*recent* history is the only true history.
