---
title: "Decision Trees, Bagging & Random Forests"
summary: "Splitting on features, and averaging many trees to reduce variance."
---

A decision tree is a model that *asks a series of yes/no questions*
about the features and returns a prediction at the leaf: "is income
above 50k? is the account age under a year? then predict default." It
is the most human-legible model there is, a flowchart, and its
ensemble (the **random forest**) is one of the strongest classical
models for tabular data. The pair teaches two big ideas: **recursive
partitioning** and **ensembling**.

## The decision tree

Training a tree is *recursive splitting*: at each node, choose the
feature and threshold that best separates the labels (the split that
reduces "impurity" most, the [greedy](/dsa/patterns/greedy-algorithms)
choice), partition the data, and recurse on each side until the leaves
are pure or a stopping rule fires. The result is a set of
axis-aligned rectangular regions, each with a prediction.

Why they are loved: **interpretable** (you can read the rules, the
reason they appear in regulated domains), **no preprocessing needed**
(no feature scaling, handles mixed types and non-linearities
natively), and **capture interactions** (a deep tree represents "this
AND that" naturally). Why they are limited: a single deep tree
**overfits badly** (it can memorize the training set by splitting
until every example is alone: high
[variance](/ai-ml/concepts/overfitting-and-bias-variance)), and small
data changes can produce a *very different* tree (unstable). The fix
is not to make one tree better: it is to make *many* and average.

## Bagging: averaging to reduce variance

**Bootstrap aggregating (bagging)**: train many trees, each on a
*different random sample* of the data (drawn with replacement, a
"bootstrap" sample), and *average* their predictions. The insight:
each tree overfits *differently*, and averaging independent errors
*cancels* them: variance drops, while bias stays the same. This is
the [ensembling](/ai-ml/concepts/regularization) idea, and it is why
a *collection of overfit trees* beats a single carefully-pruned one:
diversity plus averaging is the regularizer.

## Random forests: bagging plus feature randomness

The **random forest** adds one more source of diversity: at each
split, consider only a *random subset* of features (not all of them).
This decorrelates the trees further (without it, every tree would
latch onto the same dominant feature, and averaging correlated errors
helps less), so the ensemble is even more robust. The result is one
of the best *off-the-shelf* models for tabular data: strong accuracy,
resistant to overfitting (relative to a single tree), handles missing
values and mixed types, and gives *feature importances* for free
(which features the forest relies on, the
[interpretability](/ai-ml/concepts/interpretability) surface).

## The two ideas, generalized

- **Recursive partitioning** (the tree) is the basis for the
  [gradient-boosting](/ai-ml/concepts/gradient-boosting) family and
  many interpretable models.
- **Bagging** (averaging diverse models) is the basis for random
  forests and a general variance-reduction tool (it works for *any*
  high-variance model, not just trees).

The honest modern verdict: on **tabular data**, tree ensembles
(random forests and especially gradient boosting) remain the models
to beat: deep learning's edge is on *perception* (images, audio,
text), not on tables. Knowing the tree family is knowing the
tabular-data default.
