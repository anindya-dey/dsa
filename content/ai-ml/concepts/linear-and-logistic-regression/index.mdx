title: "Linear & Logistic Regression"
summary: "The two simplest models, and why they remain baselines worth knowing."
---

Before neural networks, there were two models so simple they are
almost embarrassing — and so useful they remain the first thing to
try and the baseline to beat. **Linear regression** predicts a
*number* as a weighted sum of the inputs; **logistic regression**
predicts a *probability* (and thus a class) by squashing that sum
through a curve. Together they are the foundation every other model
is measured against, and understanding them makes the rest of ML
legible.

## Linear regression: a weighted sum

The model: `prediction = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + ... + b` — multiply each
feature by a learned weight, add them up, add a bias. Training finds
the weights that minimize the
[squared error](/ai-ml/concepts/loss-functions-and-risk). It is the
"fit a line (or hyperplane) through the data" idea.

Why it matters beyond its simplicity: **it is interpretable** (each
weight says how much that feature moves the prediction, holding the
others fixed — the reason it is used in domains that require
explanation, like credit scoring), **it is fast** (training is a
closed-form or convex optimization — no local minima, no tuning), and
**it is the baseline** (if a fancy model does not beat linear
regression, the fancy model is not earning its complexity). Its
limitation is obvious: it can only capture *linear* relationships,
and real data is usually not linear.

## Logistic regression: a probability, then a class

For classification, the same weighted sum is passed through a
**sigmoid** (a curve that squashes any number into `(0, 1)`), giving
a *probability*: `p = sigmoid(w·x + b)`. Predict "class 1" if `p >
0.5`, and you have a classifier. Training minimizes
[cross-entropy](/ai-ml/concepts/loss-functions-and-risk) (the loss
that punishes confident wrong answers), and the result is a *linear
decision boundary* with *calibrated-ish* probabilities.

Two things make it more than a toy: **the output is a probability**
(so it feeds decisions — the [calibration](/ai-ml/systems/evaluation-metrics)
that CTR models need, and the reason logistic regression was the ad
industry's workhorse for years), and **the coefficients are
odds-ratios** (interpretable as "each unit of feature x multiplies the
odds by e^w", the language of risk models). It is the default
*baseline classifier*, and for many problems it is *enough*.

## The shared lesson: linear models and feature engineering

Both models are *linear in the features* — which means their power
depends entirely on *which features you give them*. A logistic
regression on raw pixels cannot recognize a digit; the same model on
*engineered* features (or on [learned
embeddings](/ai-ml/concepts/word-embeddings)) can. This is the
central lesson the linear models teach: **the model finds the
weights; you (or a deeper model) must supply the representation.**
Deep learning is, in one framing, the automation of that feature
engineering — the network *learns* the nonlinear features that a
linear model on top can use.

## Where they fit today

Linear and logistic regression are not obsolete: they are the *first
baseline* (fast, interpretable, hard to beat on small or tabular
data), the *final layer* of many deep models (a neural network often
ends in a logistic regression), and the *interpretable* option where
explanation is required. Knowing them is knowing what "simple" means
in ML — the yardstick.
