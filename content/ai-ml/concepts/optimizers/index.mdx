[Gradient descent](/ai-ml/concepts/gradient-descent) says "step
downhill." The **optimizer** decides *how*: how big the step, whether
to remember previous steps, whether to use a different step size per
parameter. These choices, more than almost anything else, decide
whether a model trains quickly, slowly, or not at all — which is why
the optimizer is a one-line setting with an outsized effect on
results.

## The progression

- **SGD** (stochastic gradient descent, the base): step each
  parameter against the gradient, scaled by a fixed learning rate.
  Simple, and its *noise* (from mini-batches) helps escape bad spots.
  Its problem: a single learning rate for every parameter, and the
  gradient's direction is often a poor one (across a narrow valley,
  not along it).
- **Momentum**: accumulate a running average of past gradients and
  step in *that* direction — like a ball rolling downhill, it builds
  speed along consistent directions and dampens oscillation. This
  fixes the "zigzag across a ravine" problem, because the consistent
  component of the gradient accumulates while the oscillating
  component cancels.
- **RMSProp / Adagrad**: adapt the step size *per parameter* — give
  parameters with consistently small gradients a larger step, and
  those with large gradients a smaller one, by dividing by a running
  average of gradient magnitudes. This fixes the "one learning rate
  for all parameters is wrong" problem.
- **Adam** (the modern default): combines momentum (the first moment)
  with per-parameter scaling (the second moment) and adds bias
  correction. The result: fast, robust, forgiving of a poorly-chosen
  learning rate, and works well across a huge range of problems —
  which is why "just use Adam" is the standard answer.

## Why Adam dominates (and its caveats)

Adam's appeal: **it works without much tuning** (a good default
learning rate, and it handles differently-scaled parameters
gracefully), and it converges fast on most problems. Its caveats:
it sometimes **generalizes slightly worse** than well-tuned SGD with
momentum (a real, debated result — SGD's noise can find flatter
minima), and it uses more memory (it stores two running averages per
parameter, which matters at billions of parameters). The practical
verdict: Adam for most things, SGD-with-momentum when squeezing the
last bit of generalization on a large, well-understood training run.

## The knobs that still matter

- **Learning rate**: the single most important hyperparameter, even
  with Adam. Too high diverges, too low crawls, and *schedules*
  (decay it over training, or warm it up — the reason "warmup" appears
  in every large-model recipe) matter a lot.
- **Weight decay**: the [L2
  penalty](/ai-ml/concepts/regularization), handled slightly
  differently in Adam (AdamW's decoupled decay) — the reason "AdamW"
  is the current default.
- **Batch size interacts with the learning rate**: larger batches
  give lower-noise gradients, which usually wants a larger rate (the
  "linear scaling" rule — a systems-relevant coupling, since batch
  size is also a memory/throughput decision).
