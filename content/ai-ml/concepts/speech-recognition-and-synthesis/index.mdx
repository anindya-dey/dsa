---
title: "Speech Recognition & Synthesis"
summary: "Turning audio into text and text into audio: ASR and TTS, conceptually."
---

Speech is a *sequence* problem with a twist: the input (audio) and the
output (text) are different lengths with no clean alignment ("hello"
might span half a second of sound, and one phoneme spans many audio
frames). Two mirrored tasks: **automatic speech recognition (ASR)**
— audio in, text out (dictation, voice assistants, transcription) —
and **text-to-speech (TTS)** — text in, audio out (reading aloud,
voice interfaces). Both have been transformed by deep learning, and
both illustrate the sequence and multimodal ideas from this track.

## The audio representation

Audio is a waveform (pressure over time), but models rarely work on
the raw waveform — they use a **spectrogram** (a picture of which
frequencies are present over time, computed via the Fourier transform)
or **learned audio features** (neural audio codecs that compress
speech into discrete tokens, much like a
[tokenizer](/ai-ml/systems/tokenizer-lld) for text). The spectrogram
is essentially an *image*, which is why vision architectures (CNNs,
then transformers) transfer naturally to audio.

## ASR: audio to text

The challenge: the alignment problem above, plus noise, accents, and
homophones. The classic architecture combined an **acoustic model**
(sounds → phonemes) with a **language model** (which word sequences
are likely — "recognize speech" vs "wreck a nice beach"). Modern
systems are **end-to-end**: a
[transformer](/ai-ml/concepts/transformers) (or a
[CNN/RNN](/ai-ml/concepts/recurrent-neural-networks) hybrid, or a
[CTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectionist_temporal_classification)-based
model) maps audio directly to text, learning the alignment implicitly.
The trend mirrors NLP: hand-designed pipelines (acoustic + language
model) → end-to-end neural models → large pre-trained models
(Whisper-style) that handle many languages and noisy conditions.

## TTS: text to audio

The reverse, and historically harder to make *natural*. The stages:
text → **linguistic features** (pronunciation, prosody — where the
stress goes) → **acoustic features** (the spectrogram) → **waveform**
(the actual sound). Classic systems used concatenative synthesis
(stitch together recorded snippets) or parametric models (statistical
models of the features). Modern neural TTS is **end-to-end**:
sequence-to-sequence or [diffusion](/ai-ml/concepts/diffusion-models)
models generate the spectrogram, and a **neural vocoder** (a network
that turns spectrograms into waveforms) produces the audio. This is
what makes modern voices natural — and what enables **voice cloning**
and **zero-shot TTS** (synthesizing a new speaker's voice from a few
seconds of audio).

## The unifying view

Both tasks are **sequence transduction** — transform one sequence into
another — which is exactly the
[seq2seq](/ai-ml/concepts/sequence-to-sequence) pattern, with audio on
one side. And the modern "speech language model" trend fuses them:
tokenize audio, treat it like text, and train one
[transformer](/ai-ml/concepts/transformers) to handle speech, text,
and translation — the reason today's voice assistants are converging
with large language models. The concept ties the sequence track and
the multimodal track together: **speech is just another modality to
tokenize and model**.
