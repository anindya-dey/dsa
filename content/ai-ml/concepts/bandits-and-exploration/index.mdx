summary: "Choosing under uncertainty with partial feedback: the exploration/exploitation trade behind ranking and experimentation."

You have ten headlines and one slot on the front page. Which do you show?
You could run an [A/B test](/ai-ml/systems/ab-testing-experimentation):
split traffic evenly, wait two weeks, and promote the winner. Or you
could watch clicks as they happen: try each headline a little, keep
whichever does best in front of more readers, and adjust continuously.
That second option is a **multi-armed bandit**, and the difference is
what it does with the losers of the world: it stops showing them while
it still has the chance to show something better.

The name comes from casinos: rows of slot machines ("one-armed
bandits"), each with an unknown payout rate. You have a budget of plays.
Do you stick with the machine that has paid best so far, or try others
in case one is better? [Reinforcement
learning](/ai-ml/concepts/reinforcement-learning-basics) calls this
**exploration versus exploitation**, and the bandit is its cleanest
form: one decision, repeated, with no long-game strategy complicating
it. What makes the problem hard is **partial feedback**: you see the
reward only for the arm you pulled. The nine headlines you did not show
might have done better, and you will never know.

## The problem, formally

A bandit is `k` arms with unknown average rewards. Each round: pull an
arm, observe its reward. Maximize the total over all rounds. The gap
between your accumulated reward and the best arm's is **regret**, and
regret is the honest measure: a good policy does not pick the right arm
immediately, it *converges* to it while wasting few plays on bad arms.

The tension is provable: commit to the current favorite too early and
you may lock in a mediocre arm forever (no exploration); keep sampling
everything forever and you keep paying for known-bad arms (no
exploitation). Every policy is a rule for balancing those two errors.

## The classic policies

**Epsilon-greedy** is the policy you would write yourself: exploit the
best-known arm, except with probability ε, try a random one. Simple,
and it wastes exploration uniformly on *known-bad* arms forever, which
is why it is the baseline rather than the answer.

**Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)** fixes that waste with a principle:
**optimism under uncertainty**. Score each arm as "best it could
plausibly be, given what I have seen": an arm tried rarely has a wide
plausibility interval, so its *upper* bound stays high, and it keeps
getting a chance until the data narrows. Arms proven bad drop out on
their own. Play the arm with the highest upper bound, and exploration
goes exactly where uncertainty lives, the policy that earns confidence
instead of assuming it.

**Thompson sampling** takes the Bayesian route: keep a probability
distribution over each arm's reward, sample one plausible value per arm
per round, and play the arm whose *sampled* value is highest. Uncertain
arms produce wildly varying samples, so they keep occasionally winning;
well-measured arms win exactly as often as they are actually best. It
is a small amount of bookkeeping with excellent empirical behavior,
and the one most production systems land on.

## Contextual bandits

The plain bandit assumes everyone is the same, which no audience is.
The **contextual bandit** sees a context (the user, the query, the
device) before choosing, and learns a policy from context to arm.
"Show the sports headline to sports readers" is not learnable by a
context-free bandit. This is the bandit that matches reality, and it is
no longer simple statistics: the arm-selection function is a model, a
[regression](/ai-ml/concepts/linear-and-logistic-regression) or an
ensemble, trained on (context, arm, reward) logs.

Contextual bandits are quietly everywhere:

- **News and feed ranking**: articles are arms, the user is context,
  the click is the reward, and the
  [recommendation system](/system-design/hld/recommendation-system) is
  the bandit with a warehouse behind it.
- **Ad and offer selection**: the [CTR-ranking](/ai-ml/systems/ctr-ad-ranking)
  world, where every impression is a pull.
- **Experimentation that never ends**: A/B tests end; bandits adapt, and
  on slow-moving questions (which plan should the pricing page lead
  with?) the always-on bandit is the better instrument.

## Bandits versus A/B tests

The choice is about what you want to know.

An A/B test buys *statistical knowledge*: a clean, unbiased answer to
"which arm is better", with confidence, for a fixed cost in traffic.
A bandit buys *accumulated reward*: more total value during the
experiment, because it stops spending traffic on losers, at the price
of a messier answer (the assignment was adaptive, so the estimate is
biased toward the arms you explored early). The rule of thumb: run A/B
when the decision needs to be *proven* (a launch, a headline change for
a big event) and a bandit when the decision must keep being *made*
(ongoing personalization, continuous content rotation). And note the
structural trap: adaptive assignment can bias the very metrics you
wanted to compare, the reason serious A/B analysis is randomized and
the reason bandit logs are analyzed with bandit-aware methods, not
naive averages.

## What goes wrong in production

- **Delayed feedback**: rewards that arrive hours later (a purchase) make
  every policy over-explore in the meantime, because unobserved rewards
  look like zero.
- **Non-stationary arms**: yesterday's winning headline is boring today.
  The fix is discounting or sliding windows, so the bandit *forgets*,
  or you have rebuilt
  [drift](/ai-ml/systems/concept-drift-retraining) the hard way.
- **Rich-get-richer**: exploitation collapses diversity, feedback loops
  entrench one arm, and the system stops learning anything at all.
- **Reward design**: "clicks" is not "value"; optimizing the first made
  every homepage in the world worse, the same
  [reward-design](/ai-ml/concepts/reinforcement-learning-basics) trap
  RLHF exists to manage.
