Deep networks are hard to train, and two unglamorous techniques are
most of the reason they now work: **normalization** (rescaling the
activations inside the network) and **initialization** (choosing the
starting weights carefully). Neither is conceptually deep, and both
were essential to making networks with dozens or hundreds of layers
trainable — they are the plumbing that keeps gradients healthy and
training stable.

## The problem: unstable training

Two failure modes, both about *scale*:

- **Exploding/vanishing activations** (as data flows through many
  layers, the outputs can grow huge or shrink to nothing — the
  gradients follow, and training stalls or diverges).
- **Bad initialization** (if weights start too large, activations
  explode; too small, they vanish; and either way the network starts
  in a bad region and struggles to recover).

The fix is to *control the scale* of activations and weights at every
step, which is what normalization and initialization do.

## Initialization: start in a good place

The old default (small random weights) does not scale to depth. The
principled approaches set the initial weight *variance* so that the
activations' variance stays roughly constant through the layers —
**Xavier/Glorot** (for tanh/sigmoid-like activations) and **He**
(for ReLU) initialization. The idea: choose the starting scale so
signal neither amplifies nor attenuates as it passes through the
network, giving gradients a healthy starting point. It is a
one-line detail with a real effect on whether training converges.

## Normalization: keep activations stable

**Normalization layers** rescale activations *during* training, so
each layer sees inputs with a stable distribution (roughly
zero-mean, unit-variance) regardless of how the previous layer's
scale drifted:

- **BatchNorm** (normalize across the *batch*: each feature is
  rescaled using the mean/variance over the examples in the batch).
  It stabilizes and accelerates training dramatically, and its
  batch statistics add a little noise (a mild
  [regularizer](/ai-ml/concepts/regularization)). Its catch: it
  depends on the *batch*, so it behaves awkwardly with small batches
  or variable-length sequences.
- **LayerNorm** (normalize across the *features* of a single example:
  no dependence on the batch). This is what
  [transformers](/ai-ml/concepts/transformers) use, because sequence
  models have variable batch structure and cannot rely on batch
  statistics — the reason LayerNorm is the default in modern
  architectures.
- **RMSNorm** (a simplified LayerNorm): cheaper, and now common in
  large language models.

The intuition: normalization keeps every layer in the "well-behaved"
regime (activations neither exploding nor vanishing), so gradients
flow and training is stable — the reason it became standard.

## Residual connections (the third stabilizer)

Worth naming alongside: **residual connections** ("skip connections":
a layer's output is *added* to its input rather than replacing it)
give gradients a direct path backward, which is what makes *very*
deep networks (ResNets, transformers, hundreds of layers) trainable.
Where normalization keeps activations scaled, residuals keep the
gradient path short — together they are why "deep" stopped being a
barrier.

## Why it matters

These are the techniques that turned "deep networks are hard to
train" into "deep networks train reliably", and they are why the
architectures that dominate today (transformers, ResNets) use them by
default. The lesson: **architecture alone is not enough; training
stability is engineered** — through initialization, normalization,
and skip connections, the unglamorous engineering that makes the
glamorous models work.
