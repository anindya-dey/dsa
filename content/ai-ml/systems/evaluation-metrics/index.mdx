A metric is a **choice about what "good" means**, made explicit, and
every serious ML incident traces back to a metric that measured the
wrong thing confidently. This page is the metric vocabulary and,
more importantly, the *failure mode of each*, because the systems
designer's job is to pick the metric whose failure mode the product
can survive.

## The confusion matrix, the root of everything

Binary classification's four outcomes: true positive, true negative,
false positive, false negative. Every threshold-based metric is a
function of these four numbers, and the first design decision is
*which error is worse*, the question no metric answers for you.

- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): of the things we flagged, how many
  were right. The metric of *false-alarm cost* (a spam filter
  blocking a real email: precision).
- **Recall** = TP / (TP + FN): of the things that mattered, how many
  we caught. The metric of *missed-cost* (a fraud system letting a
  charge through: recall).
- **F1** = the harmonic mean: one number for "both matter," which is
  a *choice* to weight them equally, worth stating as one (the
  product rarely weights them equally: the
  [fraud design](/system-design/hld/fraud-detection)'s dollar
  arithmetic is the honest version).

The precision/recall *trade* is a **threshold** (lower the bar:
more flags, higher recall, lower precision), which is why "we
improved the model" must say *at which operating point*: a model
can dominate another at every threshold (a better
[ROC](/ai-ml/systems/evaluation-metrics) curve) or only win
at one.

## Ranking and probability metrics

- **ROC-AUC** (the area under the true-positive-vs-false-positive
  curve): the probability a random positive ranks above a random
  negative, **threshold-independent**: good for model *comparison*,
  and the classic trap on **imbalanced data** (a fraud rate of
  0.1%: a huge ROC-AUC can hide a useless model, because the vast
  negative class dominates the curve: the fix is
  **precision-recall AUC**, which the imbalanced cases actually
  care about).
- **NDCG** (normalized discounted cumulative gain, the *ranking*
  metric): the [feed](/system-design/hld/recommendation-system) and
  [search](/system-design/fundamentals/search-and-inverted-index)
  family's measure (relevance graded, position discounted: item 1
  counts more than item 10, normalized against the ideal order),
  which is why ranking products are evaluated with NDCG and not
  accuracy.
- **Calibration** (does a 0.7 mean a 70% chance?): the metric
  *nobody* checks and *everyone* needs when scores feed decisions
  (a [bid](/system-design/hld/ad-click-aggregator) priced by the
  model's probability: miscalibration is a money bug), measured by
  reliability diagrams and fixed by
  [Platt scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platt_scaling) /
  [isotonic regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotonic_regression).
- **Regression's metrics** (MAE, RMSE, MAPE, quantile loss): RMSE
  punishes large errors (the outlier-sensitive choice), MAE the
  average (robust), and the choice is a *product* statement about
  whether big misses matter disproportionately.

## The system-level metrics (the ones that actually ship)

The offline metric is a proxy; the product cares about **business
outcomes**, and the [two-tier evaluation](/ai-ml/systems/offline-vs-online-evaluation)
exists because the two diverge:

- **Engagement** (clicks, watch time, session length): the
  [feed](/system-design/hld/recommendation-system) metrics, and the
  ones [A/B tests](/ai-ml/systems/ab-testing-experimentation)
  measure directly.
- **Cost/latency** (the model's own serving bill and p99: a metric
  the ML team often forgets, and the
  [serving pages](/ai-ml/systems/model-serving-architectures)
  make first-class).
- **Guardrails** (the metrics that must *not* regress: diversity,
  safety, latency: the [A/B](/ai-ml/systems/ab-testing-experimentation)
  discipline's "guardrail metrics," which turn a metric suite into
  a *contract*).

## The traps, collected

- **Class imbalance** (accuracy is a lie on imbalanced data: "99%
  accurate" on a 1% fraud rate is the all-negative predictor,
the first thing to check, always).
- **The wrong split** (a metric measured on leaked or
  non-temporal data: the [training pipeline's](/ai-ml/systems/training-data-pipelines)
  leakage family, which produces *excellent* metrics and worthless
  models).
- **Aggregate hiding a segment** (95% overall accuracy, 60% on the
  minority class: the [fairness](/ai-ml/systems/bias-fairness-explainability)
  page's whole subject, and the reason "break the metric down by
  segment" is the first diagnostic).
- **Optimizing the metric, not the goal** (Goodhart's law, ML
  edition: a model trained hard on NDCG games the ranking; the
  product wanted *retention*, and the
  [feedback loop](/system-design/hld/recommendation-system) drifted
  the system toward the metric's shape).
