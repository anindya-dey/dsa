---
title: "Hyperparameter Tuning at Scale"
summary: "Searching the training configuration space without wasting the compute budget."
---

Every model has **hyperparameters** — the settings chosen *before*
training (learning rate, batch size, layers, regularization, the
feature set) — and their values matter *enormously* (a wrong
learning rate means a model that never converges). **Hyperparameter
tuning** is the search for good values, and its systems problem is
stark: each evaluation is a *full training run* (hours, GPUs,
money), so the search must be *smart* (find good configs in few
runs) and *efficient* (use the fleet well). It is the
[experiment-tracking](/ai-ml/systems/experiment-tracking)
loop, driven automatically, under a budget.

## The search strategies, priced

- **Grid search** (try every combination on a fixed grid): simple,
  embarrassingly parallel, and *wasteful* (the cost is exponential
  in the number of hyperparameters, and most of the grid is
  low-value: the first thing to *not* do, at scale).
- **Random search** (sample configurations randomly): the classic
  finding — *random beats grid* for the same budget, because only a
  few hyperparameters matter and random search covers their range
  better (the [capacity-planning](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
  intuition: cover the *important* dimensions densely, not the
  full grid).
- **Bayesian optimization** (build a *surrogate model* of
  "hyperparameters → performance" and choose the next configuration
  to *maximize expected improvement*: the smart search — fewer runs,
  better results — at the cost of a *sequential* loop (each choice
  depends on the previous results: less parallelizable than random,
  which is the trade)).
- **Successive halving / Hyperband** (the *budget* strategy: run
  many configs *briefly*, keep the promising half, give them more
  budget, repeat — the [tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperband)
  that *prunes* bad configs early, so the fleet's compute goes to
  the configs that might win. The multi-fidelity idea: train on a
  *subset* first (fewer epochs, less data), and only the survivors
  get the full run — the biggest practical saving, and the standard
  at scale).
- **Population-based training** (evolve the hyperparameters *during*
  training (mutate the best runs, replace the worst): the
  [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_algorithm) flavor,
  for the expensive runs where restarting is unaffordable).

## The systems design

The tuning *is* a [job scheduler](/system-design/hld/distributed-job-scheduler)
problem, and the design decisions are the scheduler's:

- **Many parallel trials** (the fleet runs N training runs
  concurrently: the [distributed
  training](/ai-ml/systems/distributed-training) resources,
  times N, which is why tuning is the *costliest* ML activity and
  the reason the budget strategies matter).
- **The trial lifecycle** (spawn → train → evaluate → report: each
  trial is a [job](/system-design/hld/distributed-job-scheduler)
  (checkpointable, retryable), and the *controller* (the search
  algorithm) decides the *next* trials from the reported results —
  the [experiment-tracking](/ai-ml/systems/experiment-tracking)
  loop, closed).
- **Early stopping and pruning** (a trial that is clearly losing is
  *killed* (the successive-halving idea): the
  [backpressure](/system-design/fundamentals/backpressure-and-flow-control)
  discipline, freeing the GPU for a promising config — the single
  biggest *cost* lever, because most trials are losers and killing
  them early is most of the saving).
- **Resource matching** (the *trial* is a training run: small
  configs need a GPU, big ones need a node — the tuning controller
  must *size* each trial, and the [GPU
  scheduler](/ai-ml/systems/gpu-accelerator-management)
  places it, the [capacity-planning](/system-design/fundamentals/capacity-planning-and-estimation)
  match, automated).

## The honest trade

The page's real lesson: **the tuning budget is the design
variable.** More trials = better configs = more compute; the
question is *where on the curve* to stop, and the answer is the
[cost-optimization](/ai-ml/systems/cost-optimization-ml)
arithmetic (the marginal gain of another trial vs. its cost) —
which is why the mature answer is *not* "tune exhaustively" but
"tune *enough*, on the *important* hyperparameters, with *early
pruning*, and stop." The failure modes on both sides: too little
tuning (a bad config ships and the whole model underperforms), too
much (weeks of GPUs for a 0.2% gain that the eval noise cannot even
resolve: the [evaluation](/ai-ml/systems/evaluating-llm-systems)
discipline, telling you when further tuning is *measuring noise*).
