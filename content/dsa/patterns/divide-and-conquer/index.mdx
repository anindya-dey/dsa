title: "Divide & Conquer"
summary: "Split the input in half, solve each half recursively, and combine: the shape behind merge sort, quickselect, closest pair, and half of binary search."
---

Divide and conquer is [recursion](/dsa/patterns/recursion-and-math) where
the smaller problem is not "the input minus one element" but **the input
cut in half**. That single change transforms the cost profile: shaving
one element gives depth n, and halving gives depth log n, which is the
difference between linear-with-exponential-branching and the entire
O(n log n) family. Merge sort, quicksort, quickselect, closest pair,
karatsuba multiplication, and FFT are all this one shape: **split into
(hopefully equal) halves, recurse, combine the results**.

The pattern's discipline is that each of the three steps has its own
quality bar, and the bugs live in a specific one of them.

## The three steps, and where the bugs live

**Divide** the input into smaller instances, ideally *equal* halves.
The classic trap: if the split is lopsided (say, one side has 1 element
and the other n - 1), the depth is n, not log n, and the algorithm quietly
degrades to linear-depth. Quick sort's worst case is exactly this: a
pivot that splits nothing. The divide step's job is to make the halves
balanced, which is pivot selection, midpoint selection, and everything
the word "balanced" means in balanced trees.

**Conquer** each half with a recursive call. The discipline from
[recursion](/dsa/patterns/recursion-and-math) applies in full: trust
the call, do not re-verify, and do not call twice on the same half.
The "one call, used twice" rule is the difference between O(log n) and
O(n) in [fast exponentiation](/dsa/patterns/recursion-and-math/pow-x-n-recursive),
and between O(n log n) and O(n²) in naive recursion-tree merges.

**Combine** the results of the halves. This is where the actual
algorithmic content lives, and where the bugs live too: merge sort's
merge (a linear weave of two sorted lists),
[quickselect's](/dsa/patterns/sorting-and-selection) *non*-combination
(throw away the half you do not need, which is the pattern's biggest
saving), closest pair's cross-boundary check (the only genuinely hard
combine step in the standard repertoire).

## How to spot it

- The input has a natural **midpoint**, and the problem for "both
  halves" relates to the problem for the whole.
- Sorting, selection, closest-pair, convex hull, or "count inversions"
  (a sorted-half question).
- A brute force is quadratic, and sorting the input would help: merge
  sort's merge is *also* the answer to counting pairs across a split,
  which is why "sort, and solve on the way up" is the pattern's most
  common disguise.
- The recurrence `T(n) = 2 T(n/2) + f(n)` matches.

## The recurrence and the Master Theorem

Every divide-and-conquer algorithm costs out to
`T(n) = a * T(n / b) + f(n)`: a subproblems each of size n / b, plus
f(n) work to divide and combine. The **Master Theorem** prices the whole
family in one comparison: the work at the leaves is
`n^(log_b a)` (the number of base-case-sized subproblems), and if the
combine work f(n) dominates, the total is f(n); if the leaves dominate,
the total is the leaf count; if they tie (f(n) = n^(log_b a) * log^k n),
you pay both plus a log.

Two instantiations cover most real usage:

- Merge sort: `T(n) = 2 T(n/2) + O(n)`; leaves are `n^1`, combine is
  `n^1`, a tie: **O(n log n)**.
- Binary search: `T(n) = T(n/2) + O(1)`; one half, constant work, leaves
  are `n^0`: **O(log n)**. (Binary search is divide and conquer with the
  combine step deleted, which is why the
  [binary search page](/dsa/patterns/binary-search-variants) is its own
  pattern.)
- Quickselect: `T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n)`; the discarded half: **O(n)**
  expected, the whole of selection's advantage.

## The counting trick: solve on the way up

The pattern's most useful disguise: many "count pairs with property P"
problems become divide and conquer because *sorted halves answer pair
questions in linear time*. Count inversions is the canonical example:
split, recursively count inversions within each half (that is the
recursion), and count *cross* inversions during the merge, which a
two-pointer sweep over the sorted halves does in linear time. The merge
step is doing double duty (it sorts *and* it counts), and that doubling
is why the algorithm is O(n log n) rather than a sort plus a separate
quadratic count.

The same shape answers "count pairs (i, j) with a[i] + a[j] > x", "count
smaller elements after self" (which this site covers with a
[Fenwick tree](/dsa/patterns/range-query-structures/count-of-smaller-numbers-after-self),
the other tool for the same question), and reverse-pair counting. The
recognition move is: **if sorting the halves would make the pair question
trivial, and you were going to need sorted data anyway, merge-sort with
the counting folded into the merge**.

## When it doesn't fit

If the problem cannot be split into independent halves (the answer for
the left half depends on the right half's *contents*, not just its
result), divide and conquer dies, and that is exactly where
[DP](/dsa/patterns/dynamic-programming) (overlapping subproblems, shared
state) or a [sweep](/dsa/patterns/intervals-and-sweep-line) takes over.
If the combine step costs more than linear (having to compare every
cross pair genuinely), the log-depth advantage can evaporate: closest
pair survives it by a packing argument (only a constant number of
cross-candidates can be near the line), and *most* cross-pair problems do
not. And if there is no natural ordering or midpoint, the "split" is
arbitrary and the halves are not really subproblems of the same shape.

## The takeaway

Split in half, recurse with trust, combine with care, and price with the
Master Theorem. The combine step is the algorithm (merge, or discard a
half, or a cross-check), the divide step's job is balance, and the
recursion is just the machinery. When a pair-counting problem smells
quadratic, ask what sorted halves would make linear, because that is the
pattern's favorite disguise.
